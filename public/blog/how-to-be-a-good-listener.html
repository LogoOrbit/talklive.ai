How to Be a Good Listener, Even With Someone You Just Met

Listening is the underrated half of every good conversation. How to show you are really listening on a voice call, the habits that quietly shut people down, and what to do when you run out of things to say.

By the TalkLive team · Updated 2026-09-23

Most advice about conversations is about what to say: better openers, better questions, better stories. But ask people what made a conversation memorable and they rarely mention the other person's clever lines. They say, "They really listened."

Listening well is a skill, and it is one you can get noticeably better at in a single evening. It matters even more on a voice call, where there is no nodding or eye contact to show you are paying attention.

Why listening is harder than it sounds

We think faster than people talk, so our minds wander into the gap. Very often that gap gets filled with planning what we will say next, which means we are waiting for our turn rather than listening.

The other person can hear it. Replies that come a little too fast, that change the subject, or that turn the story back to ourselves all signal that we were not really following. With strangers, where there is no shared history to fall back on, that signal ends conversations quickly.

Show you are listening, out loud

On a call, your listening has to be audible. Small sounds - "mm", "right", "oh wow", "no way" - tell the other person you are still there and still interested. Silence on a phone line reads as distraction.

Reflect back what you heard. "So you moved there for work and ended up staying?" shows you followed the story and gives the other person an easy way to continue. It is the simplest listening technique there is, and one of the most effective.

Ask about the feeling, not only the facts. "What was that like?" or "Were you nervous?" invites a real answer. "What year was that?" usually does not.

Remember one detail and use it later. If they mentioned a sister, an exam or a dog early on, bringing it up ten minutes later shows you were paying attention in a way few things can.

Habits that quietly shut people down

Topping their story. They mention a long day; you describe a longer one. It feels like relating, but it takes the conversation away from them.

Jumping to advice. Unless someone asks what they should do, they usually want to be heard, not fixed. "That sounds really frustrating" often helps more than a solution.

Interrupting to agree. Even enthusiastic interruptions cut the other person off. Letting them finish, then responding, sounds far more interested.

Half-listening while scrolling or typing. People can hear it in your replies. If you need to do something else, say so or mute for a moment.

Silence is not a failure

A pause of two or three seconds feels endless to the person who thinks they should fill it and perfectly natural to everyone else. Often, if you leave a short silence after someone finishes, they will add the most interesting part of what they were going to say.

If a silence really does stretch, you do not need a brilliant new topic. Go back to something they said earlier - "You mentioned you started learning guitar, how is that going?" - which also shows you were listening. There are more ideas in what to talk about with a stranger.

Listening across languages and accents

On a platform where you might talk to someone in another country, listening includes patience. If you do not catch something, ask - "Sorry, could you say that again?" is friendlier than guessing and replying to the wrong thing.

If the other person is speaking in their second language, give them time to find the word instead of finishing their sentences. Many people use random chat specifically to practise speaking, and nothing helps them more than a patient listener. Our guide to talking to strangers in another language has more on this.

Listening has limits, and that is fine

Being a good listener does not mean putting up with anything. If a conversation turns hostile, sexual or makes you uncomfortable, you can end it at any time, and you can report anyone who breaks our Community Guidelines.

But most strangers are simply people who wanted someone to talk to tonight. Give them your full attention for ten minutes and you will be surprised how often the conversation ends with both of you saying it was the best call of the day. When you are ready to practise, start a call.
