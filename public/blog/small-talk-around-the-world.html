A conversation passport

Small talk around the world

The first sixty seconds with a stranger follow different rules in every country. Here are the greetings, the safe openers and the questions to save for later in twelve of them.

In 1923 the anthropologist Bronisław Malinowski gave a name to the kind of talk that carries no information at all: "phatic communion". Comments about the weather, "how are you" with no expectation of an answer, a remark about the queue. He argued that this talk is not empty. Its job is to create a bond, to show that you are friendly and paying attention, before anything that matters is said.

Every culture has phatic talk. What changes is the script. In one country the polite opener is the weather, in another it is food, in another it is a question about your age that would be startling anywhere else. Get the script wrong and a perfectly friendly person can seem cold, nosy or odd. Get it roughly right, even clumsily, and doors open fast.

Treat what follows as a starting point, not a rulebook. Countries are huge, generations differ, and the person in front of you is always more important than the generalisation. But when you are talking to someone from the other side of the world, a few of these will make the first minute easier.

United States

"Hey, how are you?"

Try
Work, weekend plans, sport, food, where you are from.
Save
Salary and, with strangers, politics or religion.

"How are you?" is a greeting, not a question. "Good, you?" is the full answer.

United Kingdom

"You alright?"

Try
The weather, honestly. Also travel, telly, football and mild complaints.
Save
How much things cost, and anything that sounds like boasting.

Understatement is a sport. "Not bad" can mean genuinely good.

Japan

"Konnichiwa." Online: a simple "hajimemashite" (nice to meet you).

Try
Food, seasons, travel, hobbies, anime and games if they bring them up.
Save
Blunt disagreement and very personal questions early on.

Listeners give constant small responses, "hai", "sō desu ne", called aizuchi. Silence from you can read as not listening.

China

"Nǐ hǎo." Traditionally also "Chī le ma?" (Have you eaten?)

Try
Food above all, family, studies, cities you have visited.
Save
Politics, and anything that could make someone lose face in front of others.

"Have you eaten?" is care, not a dinner invitation. Answering "yes, have you?" is perfect.

India

"Namaste", or "Hi, how are you?"

Try
Cricket, films, food, family, exams and work.
Save
Criticising religion or a region, even as a joke.

Questions about family, marriage or your job can come early. It usually means interest, not intrusion.

Nigeria

"How far?" (Pidgin) or "Good evening, how is your day?"

Try
Football, music, food, family, hustle and business.
Save
Skipping the greeting. Launching straight into business can feel rude.

Greetings are generous and specific. In Yoruba there is a greeting for almost every situation, including one for someone at work.

Egypt

"Ezzayak?" (to a man) / "Ezzayik?" (to a woman)

Try
Football, films and songs, food, family, Cairo traffic.
Save
Mocking religion, and pushing for direct yes-or-no answers.

"Inshallah" (God willing) is everywhere and can mean yes, maybe or not really. Tone tells you which.

Brazil

"Oi, tudo bem?" Answer: "Tudo bom!"

Try
Football, music, food, beaches, the city they are from.
Save
Mixing up Brazil with Spanish-speaking countries. It is Portuguese.

Warmth comes fast and conversations run long. A quick exit can feel cold.

Germany

"Hallo", "Guten Abend"

Try
Travel, work, hobbies, books, practical topics.
Save
Asking "Wie geht's?" unless you want a real answer.

Small talk is less valued than real conversation. Directness is honesty, not rudeness.

Finland

"Moi!" or "Hei!"

Try
Nature, saunas, hobbies, music, the seasons.
Save
Filling every pause. Overenthusiasm can seem insincere.

Comfortable silence is part of the conversation. Let it sit.

Philippines

"Kumusta?" Older people: add "po"

Try
Food, family, singing, basketball, where in the islands they are from.
Save
Direct confrontation, which makes people lose face.

"Kain tayo!" (Let's eat!) is often a friendly greeting. Taglish, mixing Tagalog and English, is normal.

South Korea

"Annyeonghaseyo"

Try
Food, K-dramas and music if they like them, travel, study.
Save
Being offended when asked your age.

Age is often asked early because Korean grammar changes with who is older. It is a practical question.

The three openers that work almost everywhere

Ask for a recommendation. "What should I eat if I ever visit?" or "What song should I listen to from there?" lets the other person be the expert, which most people enjoy, and it sidesteps every sensitive topic at once.

Try their language, badly. A greeting in someone's own language, even mispronounced, nearly always gets a smile. Ask them to correct you and you have a conversation.

Ask about their time of day. When you talk to someone on the other side of the world, you are often in different parts of the day: your morning, their midnight. "What time is it there? What are you doing up?" is a natural opener with a story behind it.

The universal "save for later" list

Politics, religion and money are the classic three, and they are a sensible default with someone you have just met. The interesting part is everything in between. Age, marriage and salary are rude in some places and routine in others. The safest rule is to let the other person set the depth: answer what you are comfortable answering, ask back at the same level, and change the subject gracefully when you would rather not.

And when you get it wrong, which everyone does, the fix is the same in every country. Smile, say "sorry, I didn't know", and ask how it works where they are. Curiosity forgives a lot. Which of these twelve would you most like to try on a real person tonight?

Questions about talking across cultures

What are safe small talk topics in any country?

Food, travel, music, films, sport and where someone is from work almost everywhere. Asking what a person recommends, a dish, a song or a place, is a near-universal opener because it lets them be the expert.

What topics should I avoid with strangers from other cultures?

Politics, religion and money are the safest to leave until you know someone, though the exact line varies. Personal questions about age, marriage or salary are normal in some cultures and intrusive in others, so take the lead from the other person.

Why do people in some countries ask about age or marriage so quickly?

In places like South Korea, age decides how people speak to each other, so it is a practical question. In India and many other countries, questions about family are a way of showing interest. They are rarely meant as judgement.

How do I start a conversation with someone from another country online?

Greet them in their language if you can, even badly; it almost always gets a smile. Then ask about something they know and you do not: their city, their food, their favourite local word.
