Loneliness is not the same as being alone.

Some of the loneliest people you know are rarely by themselves. Some of the least lonely live alone and like it. The difference turns out to matter a great deal for what helps.

Researchers tend to define loneliness as a gap: the distance between the connection you have and the connection you want. Not the number of people around you. The gap. That is why a student in a crowded dormitory can be desperately lonely, and why a retired widower who sees one close friend twice a week can be fine.

It is also why "just get out more" is such frustrating advice. It treats loneliness as a shortage of people. Often it is something else.

I

Why it gets taken seriously now

In 2023 the United States Surgeon General, Vivek Murthy, published an advisory calling loneliness and isolation an epidemic. Much of its weight came from a large review published in 2010 by Julianne Holt-Lunstad and colleagues, which pooled 148 studies covering more than 300,000 people. Those with stronger social relationships had about a 50 percent greater likelihood of survival over the study periods than those with weaker ones.

You will often see this turned into a comparison: that loneliness is as harmful as smoking fifteen cigarettes a day. It is a memorable line, and it points at something real, but it is a rough comparison of effect sizes from different kinds of studies. The careful version is simply that social connection is strongly linked with health and lifespan, on a scale worth taking seriously.

II

The cruel part

The psychologist John Cacioppo spent much of his career on a strange feature of loneliness: it tends to make people more alert to social threat. A lonely brain, his work suggested, starts scanning for rejection. Neutral faces look a little colder. A slow reply looks like a snub. A cancelled plan looks like proof.

Loneliness asks you to reach out, then makes reaching out feel dangerous.

That loop explains why loneliness can be so sticky. The very state that makes you want connection also makes you more guarded, more likely to read people uncharitably and more likely to withdraw before you can be hurt. From the outside, it can look like someone does not want company. From the inside, it feels like self-protection.

III

What the evidence says helps

In 2011, a team led by Christopher Masi reviewed the studies of interventions that had tried to reduce loneliness. They sorted them into rough types: improving social skills, increasing social support, creating more opportunities to meet people, and addressing the thinking patterns described above. The approaches that worked on those thinking patterns, noticing the assumption that others will reject you and testing it, tended to show the largest effects.

That is a surprising and hopeful finding. It suggests that for many people the bottleneck is not access to other humans. It is the filter through which other humans are seen. Some practical things follow from it:

  • Treat your predictions as guesses, not facts. "They won't want to hear from me" is a hypothesis. Send the message and see.
  • Prefer small, regular contact to rare, big events. A short weekly call does more than a party you dread for a fortnight.
  • Do things alongside people, not only with them. A class, a volunteer shift, a team. Shared tasks take the pressure off conversation.
  • Help someone. Being useful is one of the fastest routes back to feeling that you belong somewhere.
  • Count the weak ties. The shopkeeper, the neighbour, the regular at the gym. They matter more than they seem to.
IV

Where strangers fit

A conversation with a stranger is not a cure for loneliness, and it would be dishonest to say otherwise. It does not replace someone who knows you and will notice if you go quiet.

But it can be a low-stakes place to test the predictions loneliness makes. You expect the person to be cold; they are kind. You expect to have nothing to say; you talk for twenty minutes. Each time the forecast turns out wrong, it gets a little weaker. That is not nothing. For someone whose lonely brain has been telling them a story about other people, a small piece of contrary evidence can be the beginning of a different story.

So the question, if you are lonely tonight, is not only "who can I talk to?" It is also: what am I assuming they will think of me, and when did I last check?

If loneliness has turned into hopelessness, or thoughts of harming yourself, please talk to someone trained to help. In the US you can call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, Samaritans answer on 116 123. Elsewhere, findahelpline.com lists free services by country.

Sources: Office of the US Surgeon General, Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation (2023); Holt-Lunstad, Smith & Layton, PLoS Medicine (2010); Masi, Chen, Hawkley & Cacioppo, Personality and Social Psychology Review (2011); Cacioppo & Patrick, Loneliness (2008).
