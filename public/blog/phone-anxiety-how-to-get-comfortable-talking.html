"Why does my heart race every time the phone rings?"

I'm 26 and I can't use the phone. If it rings I let it ring out, then text to ask what they wanted. I once spent forty minutes writing a script to call my dentist and still hung up when the receptionist answered. I'm fine in person and I'm funny over text. My friends think it's a quirk. It's starting to cost me jobs and appointments and I feel ridiculous. Why is this so hard, and is there any way out?

Ring-shy, by email

This letter is a composite of messages we have received, edited for length.

First, you are not ridiculous, and you are far from alone. Phone anxiety is common enough to have a slightly comic nickname, "telephobia", and common enough among younger adults that some colleges and employers now offer lessons in making calls. But the more useful thing to know is that it is not irrational. Phone calls really are harder than most other kinds of conversation. Your nervous system has noticed something true and drawn the wrong conclusion from it.

Why calls really are harder

Think about what a phone call removes. In person, you can see a face: a smile tells you a joke landed; a frown tells you to explain. Over text, you can take your time, edit, delete, and send when ready. A phone call gives you neither. It is live, so there is no editing, and it is voice-only, so you get very little feedback about how you are coming across. A pause on the line could mean anything. An anxious mind fills that gap with the worst option.

There is also the ring itself. A call arrives on someone else's schedule, demanding an answer now, with no preview of what it is about. It is not surprising that it triggers a small alarm.

The habit that keeps it going

Here is the part that may be hard to hear. Your forty-minute script is not just a symptom; it is probably part of what keeps the fear alive.

Psychologists who study social anxiety talk about "safety behaviours": the things we do to get through a frightening situation, like over-rehearsing, avoiding eye contact, or letting calls go to voicemail. They feel protective. The problem is that they stop you ever learning that you would have been fine without them. When the dentist call goes well, your brain credits the script. When you let a call ring out, your brain learns that ringing phones are dangerous and avoiding them works. Each time, the fear is fed a little.

So the way out is not to get better at avoiding. It is to give your brain new evidence, in steps small enough that you can actually take them.

A ladder, not a leap

Write down a list of calls from easiest to hardest, and work up it slowly. Something like this:

  1. Call a recorded line.A cinema's showtimes, a shop's opening hours. No human, but you practise dialling and listening.
  2. Call someone who loves you, unannounced.Your mum, a close friend. Low stakes, high warmth.
  3. A thirty-second business call."Are you open on Sunday?" Write three words on a sticky note, not a script.
  4. Answer one unknown number a week.Most will be boring. That is the point.
  5. Talk to a stranger with no task at all.A conversation with no agenda is surprisingly good practice, because there is nothing to get wrong.
  6. The call you have been avoiding.By now it is just the next rung.

Stay on each rung until it feels merely dull, then move up. Expect your heart to race at first. That is not a sign it is going badly; it is the sensation of learning. It fades with repetition faster than most people expect, often within a handful of calls.

Three small tricks for the moment itself

Stand up. Many people sound and feel more confident on their feet. Say the purpose in the first sentence. "Hi, I'm calling to book a check-up." Once the other person knows why you are there, they will lead. Give yourself permission to say "sorry, could you repeat that?" Everyone does it. It is not a failure; it is how phone calls work.

When to get more help

If the fear is spreading beyond phones, into meetings, shops, or seeing friends, it may be part of wider social anxiety, which responds very well to treatment. Cognitive behavioural therapy built around exactly this kind of gradual practice has a strong track record, and a GP or doctor is a good first call. Yes, a call. You can do it from rung three.

One last thought. You say you are funny over text. That person does not disappear when you pick up the phone; they are just behind a door that fear has been holding shut. What would it feel like to let them talk out loud?
