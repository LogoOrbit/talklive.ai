Why English specifically

English is the language people most often want to practise out loud and least often get to. Classrooms teach grammar; what is missing is the unrehearsed half - being asked something you did not prepare for and answering it before you have translated it in your head.

What learners actually get stuck on

The hard part of English is almost never vocabulary. It is speed, contractions and the fact that native speakers do not pronounce half of what they write. Fifteen minutes of live conversation does more for that than an hour of exercises.

None of that is fixed by more input. It is fixed by output under time pressure, which is exactly what a call with a stranger is and what almost no study method provides.

One tip that changes everything

Say up front that you are practising. Almost nobody minds, most people slow down, and a surprising number will offer to swap - their language for yours.

The corollary: do not treat a bad call as evidence about you. Some matches are distracted, in a loud room, or simply not in the mood to be someone's practice partner. Tap Next and try again - the next one is ten seconds away.

Where English speakers are

United States - first language for most of the country, busiest around 8pm – 1am local, in four waves (UTC−5 to −10 (ET through HT)). United Kingdom - first language for most of the country, busiest around 8pm – midnight GMT (UTC+0 / UTC+1 (GMT/BST)). India - English is widely spoken here; busiest around 9pm – 2am IST (UTC+5:30 (IST)). Nigeria - first language for most of the country, busiest around 8pm – 1am WAT (UTC+1 (WAT)). Philippines - English is widely spoken here; busiest around 9pm – 2am PHT (UTC+8 (PHT)). Canada - first language for most of the country, busiest around 8pm – 1am local (UTC−3:30 to −8). Australia - first language for most of the country, busiest around 8pm – midnight AEST (UTC+8 to +11 (AWST/ACST/AEDT)). Ireland - first language for most of the country, busiest around 8pm – 1am GMT (UTC+0 / UTC+1 (GMT/IST)).

Filtering to more than one of those at once gives you a wider pool and a better spread of accents, which is what you want after the first few weeks.

Voice or text?

Both work and they train different things. Voice is the one that fixes listening speed and the terror of being put on the spot. Text is better for a language whose script you are still learning, and it is the right choice when you are somewhere you cannot talk out loud.

Most people who take this seriously use both - text to build confidence, voice once holding a sentence together stops being the hard part.

Frequently asked questions

Is practising English on TalkLive really free?

Yes. Unlimited voice calls and text chat, no card, no trial, no per-minute charge. Country filters are free too.

Will English speakers mind that I am learning?

Almost never, if you say so at the start. Most people slow down, plenty offer to swap languages, and the ones who cannot be bothered are one tap away from being replaced.

Do I need to be at a certain level to start?

No, and waiting until you feel ready is the most common mistake learners make. If you can manage “Hello” and a question, you can hold a call - the stranger will carry more of it than you expect.

Is this better than a English tutor?

It is different. A tutor gives structure and correction; random conversation gives volume, unpredictability and zero cost. The people who progress fastest use both - lessons for the grammar, strangers for the fluency.

Can I practise by text instead of speaking?

Yes. Tap to Chat gives anonymous text matching and never asks for your microphone, which is the right choice if you are still learning the script or simply cannot talk out loud right now.

How long should a English practice call be?

Ten to fifteen minutes is plenty, done often. Three short calls across a week beat one long one, because the hard part is starting cold and short calls make you practise starting.
